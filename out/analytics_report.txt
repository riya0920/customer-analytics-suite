THIS IS THE VALIDATION THAT IS ALWAYS MISSING. Segments that do not
separate FUTURE behaviour are decoration -- you can always partition a
cloud of points and give the parts names. The question is whether
membership at time T tells you anything about T+1, and the only way to
answer it is to hold out time and look.

OPERATIONALLY, what to do with a 58-point churn spread: it is a
targeting prior, not a playbook. It says where retention budget has the
most headroom, and it says nothing about what intervention works -- that
needs a test per segment. And note the reflexive problem: acting on a
segment CHANGES it, so the churn rates above are pre-intervention
baselines that stop being true the moment anyone uses them.

==============================================================================
4. THE PLANTED CHANNEL -- ATTRIBUTION IS NOT INCREMENTALITY
==============================================================================
`retargeting` has a TRUE causal effect of EXACTLY ZERO. It was targeted at
users who were already likely to convert -- which is what a real
retargeting programme does: it follows intent, it does not create it.

Naive read of the data:
  conversion rate WITH retargeting    : 0.2832
  conversion rate WITHOUT retargeting : 0.2346
  apparent 'lift'              : +0.0486  (TRUE lift: 0.0000)

What each attribution method credits it:
  last_touch         0.3578   (truth 0.0000)
  first_touch        0.0160   (truth 0.0000)
  linear             0.1463   (truth 0.0000)
  time_decay         0.2130   (truth 0.0000)
  markov_removal     0.1825   (truth 0.0000)

EVERY method credits it, including markov_removal -- which sounds causal
and is not. 'Remove the channel from the graph' is a statement about the
observed paths, not about the world: it assumes the users who saw that
channel would otherwise have walked the same graph minus one node. When
the channel was TARGETED at high-intent users, removing it also removes
the intent that arrived with it, and the method charges that intent to
the channel.

WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY SETTLE IT -- the experiment, sized:
  Design: geo holdout. Split matched markets, switch retargeting OFF in the
  control markets, leave everything else running. Primary metric is
  conversions per market; the estimand is the difference, which IS the
  incremental effect and is exactly what no observational method above
  can recover.
  Why geo and not user-level: cookie-level holdouts leak (same person,
  many devices) and the ad platform optimises delivery against the
  holdout, which breaks the randomisation.
  Cost: the foregone spend in control markets for the test duration --
  which, if the effect really is zero, is not a cost at all. That
  asymmetry is the pitch: the experiment is cheap precisely in the world
  where the channel is worthless.

  markov_removal           0.0741          187.7

Lowest attribution error: markov_removal. Best budget outcome: time_decay.

They are not the same method. Getting the CREDIT SHARES closest to
truth is not the same as making the best DECISION, because the
decision runs the shares through channel costs and a concave reach
curve. A method can be wrong in a direction that happens to be cheap
-- over-crediting a channel you were going to saturate anyway costs
nothing -- or wrong in a direction that is expensive. Choosing an
attribution method on MAE alone optimises the wrong objective; the
objective is the allocation it produces.

HANDOFF 2 -- why CLV belongs in this decision: the allocation above
maximises CONVERSIONS, and conversions are not equally valuable. Top
versus bottom CLV decile on holdout spend is $804 against $0. A
channel that acquires cheap, low-value customers can win a
conversion-based allocation and lose a value-based one. Weighting the
objective by predicted CLV per acquired customer is the join between
sections 2 and 5, and it is NOT built here -- the simulator does not
link journeys to customer ids, which is the single biggest structural
gap in this project.
