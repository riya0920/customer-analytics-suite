THE AGGREGATE-VS-INDIVIDUAL NOTE, AND THE CAVEAT ON THE CAVEAT.

The textbook warning about BG/NBD is that it RANKS populations well and
MISPREDICTS individuals -- use it to size a segment, never to decide what
one customer is worth. That warning is correct on real data.

It is NOT what this run shows. The decile table tracks closely AND the
individual correlation is 0.80, which is high. Reporting that as evidence
that the model predicts individuals well would be the single most
misleading thing in this project, because the reason is circular: the
generator in src/generate.py draws inter-purchase times from an
exponential with a Gamma-distributed rate and applies a Beta-distributed
dropout after each purchase. That IS the BG/NBD process. The model is
being scored on data that satisfies its assumptions exactly.

So what this section actually validates is that the ESTIMATOR recovers
the parameters and the calibration harness works -- which is worth
knowing and is not the same claim. On real transactions, where customers
are seasonal, subscription-like, or promotion-driven, the individual
correlation collapses and the decile table is the only part that
survives. The honest use of this build is the harness, not the number.

4. THE PLANTED CHANNEL -- ATTRIBUTION IS NOT INCREMENTALITY
==============================================================================
`retargeting` has a TRUE causal effect of EXACTLY ZERO. It was targeted at
users who were already likely to convert -- which is what a real
retargeting programme does: it follows intent, it does not create it.

Naive read of the data:
  conversion rate WITH retargeting    : 0.2738
  conversion rate WITHOUT retargeting : 0.2311
  apparent 'lift'              : +0.0428  (TRUE lift: 0.0000)

What each attribution method credits it:
  last_touch         0.3576   (truth 0.0000)
  first_touch        0.0163   (truth 0.0000)
  linear             0.1474   (truth 0.0000)
  time_decay         0.2004   (truth 0.0000)
  markov_removal     0.0000   (truth 0.0000)
  shapley            0.1684   (truth 0.0000)

4 of 6 methods give it a material share of the credit.

ONE METHOD GIVES IT ZERO, AND IT IS NOT BECAUSE IT FOUND THE
CONFOUND. markov_removal reads 0.0000 -- and it also reads 0.0000 for DISPLAY,
whose true share is 0.0889. Look at its whole row:

    markov_removal   display 0.0000  social 0.0802  email 0.2810  paid_search 0.6389  retargeting 0.0000
    TRUTH            display 0.0889  social 0.2000  email 0.3111  paid_search 0.4000  retargeting 0.0000

It has collapsed almost all the credit onto one channel and zeroed
two others. That is a known brittleness of removal effects: a
channel whose removal does not DISCONNECT the graph -- because the
remaining transitions still reach conversion -- gets a removal
effect of zero regardless of how much it contributed. It happens to
be right about retargeting and it is wrong about display for the
same reason, so the zero is an artifact and not a detection.

A method that is accidentally right is not a method you can deploy,
because you cannot tell in advance which of its zeros are correct.

The other methods behave exactly as the confound predicts. Last-touch
hands it 36% of ALL credit, because retargeting is a CLOSER -- it
fires late in journeys that were already going to convert, so it is
sitting in the last position of a great many converting paths.

SHAPLEY IS THE STRONGEST VERSION OF THIS FINDING and it is why the
second pass added it. Shapley has a formal DUMMY PLAYER axiom: a
channel that changes no coalition's conversion rate is guaranteed
exactly zero credit. It is the only method here with that property, and
a test proves it holds when the dummy channel is added AT RANDOM.

On this data Shapley gives retargeting 0.1684.

The axiom is not violated -- it is satisfied, on a coalition function
that is itself confounded. Retargeting genuinely DOES raise the observed
conversion rate of every coalition it joins, because it joins the
coalitions of customers who were going to convert. Shapley is answering
its question correctly; the question is the wrong one.

THAT IS THE WHOLE LESSON, and it is why no amount of methodological
sophistication fixes this. Attribution asks 'which touchpoints appear on
converting journeys'. Incrementality asks 'what would have happened
without this channel'. The second question cannot be answered from data
that contains no variation in whether the channel ran.

WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY SETTLE IT -- the experiment, sized:
  Design: geo holdout. Split matched markets, switch retargeting OFF in the
  control markets, leave everything else running. Primary metric is
  conversions per market; the estimand is the difference, which IS the
  incremental effect and is exactly what no observational method above
  can recover.
  Why geo and not user-level: cookie-level holdouts leak (same person,
  many devices) and the ad platform optimises delivery against the
  holdout, which breaks the randomisation.
  Cost: the foregone spend in control markets for the test duration --
  which, if the effect really is zero, is not a cost at all. That
  asymmetry is the pitch: the experiment is cheap precisely in the world
  where the channel is worthless.

confound the retargeting section is about. Weighting a budget by it
inherits that confound. The version with a causal claim needs the
experiment, and this makes the case for the experiment larger rather
than replacing it.

  TO:      CMO
  FROM:    Customer Analytics
  RE:      Marketing budget allocation, and one channel we should test

  RECOMMENDATION

  1. Stop allocating on last-touch. On our data it sends 35.8% of the
     budget -- about $536 of $1500 -- to retargeting, and our best
     evidence is that retargeting causes approximately none of the
     conversions it is credited with.

  2. Fund a geo holdout on retargeting. Switch it off in matched control
     markets for four weeks. If the effect is real we lose a month of
     incremental conversions in half our markets; if it is not, the test
     pays for itself immediately and permanently. That asymmetry is the
     argument: the experiment is cheapest in exactly the world where the
     channel is worthless.

  3. Reallocate toward paid search and email, which carry 71% of the
     measurable effect between them.

  WHAT THIS IS BASED ON

  We simulated marketing journeys with KNOWN channel effects and scored
  every standard attribution method against that truth. Under those
  conditions:

    - Every method credits a channel we know causes nothing. Last-touch
      gives it 36% of all credit; even Shapley, which is designed to
      give a useless channel exactly zero, gives it 17%.
    - The reason is not the estimators. It is that retargeting is TARGETED
      at customers who were already going to buy, so it correlates with
      conversion without causing it. No amount of modelling separates
      correlation from causation in data that contains no experiment.
    - Allocating on last-touch instead of truth costs 106 conversions
      (4.9% of achievable) on a $1500 budget.

  WHAT WE ARE NOT CLAIMING

    - These are simulated channel effects, not measured ones. What
      transfers is the RANKING of methods and the size of the error they
      make, not the specific percentages.
    - Our CLV model ranks customers well and mispredicts individuals. Use
      it to size a segment, never to decide what one customer is worth.
    - The channel-value weighting in section 6 is correlational and
      inherits the same confound. It sharpens the case for the experiment;
      it does not substitute for it.

  COST OF DOING NOTHING

    Roughly $6437 a year of budget flowing to a channel whose effect we
    have never measured, and a reported ROAS that will keep telling us it
    is working, because a channel that follows intent always looks good to
    a correlational metric.
